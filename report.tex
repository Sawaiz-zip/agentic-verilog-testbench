\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Large language models can generate Verilog testbenches from natural-language descriptions,
but the resulting code is frequently syntactically valid and functionally wrong: it
compiles, runs, reports success, and may have tested nothing. This work asks whether such
faults can be localised by static analysis before any simulation is run.

We implement a graph-based generation pipeline with a bounded repair loop and a
pre-simulation localiser performing six structural checks on the testbench and the design
together. The localiser is measured by fault injection rather than by end-to-end pass rates:
215 faults of known identity injected into testbenches known to pass. It detects 93\%,
localises 93\% to the correct fault class and signal, raises no false alarms on correct
input, and catches 30 of the 33 faults that neither the compiler nor the simulator detects.

Applied to 280 pipeline runs across two models and two circuit sets, the same localiser
reported findings on three runs in the 262 analyses in which its checks could run, and
triggered exactly one repair in the whole study; that repair cleared the finding, and a
subsequent compiler-triggered regeneration reintroduced it. Of
153 failing runs in the ablation, 133 compiled and then failed simulation.
Three alternative explanations were tested and rejected: neither a weaker nor a stronger
generator changes the structural picture, twenty benchmark circuits selected for structural
complexity produced one finding under each of them, and the localiser's correctness was
established by measurement. A fourth is not excluded: the design the localiser read was
generated alongside the testbench it checked, so the null is established for that flow and
not for one in which the design is supplied.

The faults static analysis detects have largely stopped occurring in current model output.
Two secondary results support how such claims should be evaluated: a no-diagnostics control
arm shows that uninformed regeneration is itself worth nothing ($p=1.000$ in both samples),
leaving informed repair ahead on 11 of 13 discordant circuits across 64 matched pairs at an
uncorrected $p=0.022$; and
configurations executing identical logic differ by 33 percentage points at twelve circuits
per arm and by 6.8 over the pooled 44, which bounds what a comparison at these sample sizes
can establish.
\end{abstract}

\section{Verification and the Cost of Testbenches}\label{sec:intro-cost}

A digital design is only as trustworthy as the effort spent checking it, and that effort is
substantial: surveys by the Wilson Research Group place roughly 49\% of a design engineer's
time on an IC or ASIC project in verification~\cite{wilson2022}. A large share of it goes
into writing testbenches, the programs that drive a design with stimulus and compare its
outputs against what the specification requires.

Much of that writing is transcription. Given an interface and a description of intended
behaviour, the shape of the testbench follows, and the engineer producing it is converting
one representation into another rather than making design decisions. Work of that character
is the natural target for automation. Large language models have made the attempt plausible
in a way it was not five years ago, and a body of recent work has taken it up: conversational
co-design, natural-language RTL synthesis, and iterative generation with simulator feedback,
surveyed in Section~\ref{sec:bg-llm-hdl}.

\section{How LLM-Generated Testbenches Fail}\label{sec:intro-failure}

A model will produce a syntactically valid testbench for a small design in seconds. The
difficulty is that a testbench which is syntactically valid can be functionally useless in
ways that are not apparent from looking at it. It compiles. It runs. It prints that every
scenario passed. And it may have tested nothing.

The most direct example is a testbench that instantiates the design correctly, drives every
input, and then omits to check one of the outputs. Running it does not reveal the problem,
because a check that is never performed cannot fail. The testbench passes, and it will keep
passing however many times it is run.

The standard response is to simulate and inspect the result. That works for defects
simulation can observe, and it costs a full generate--compile--simulate cycle for each
attempt. For the case above it does not work at all.

\section{Hypothesis and Research Questions}\label{sec:intro-rq}

The hypothesis behind this project is that some testbench faults are visible in the source
itself, and that a static analysis pass could therefore localise them before any simulation
runs: faster, at no token cost, and with a precise statement of which signal is at fault
rather than a wall of simulator output.

The premise is not speculative. AutoBench~\cite{qiu2024autobench}, the immediate predecessor
of this work, reports that its largest single improvement came from a mechanical
pre-simulation fix, a script inserting missing output-display statements, which raised
sequential compilation from 55.47\% to 97.33\%.

Four questions follow.

\begin{description}
\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item[\textnormal{\textbf{RQ1}}] Which categories of functional error appear most often in
LLM-generated testbenches, and which of them are detectable without simulation?
\item[\textnormal{\textbf{RQ2}}] To what extent can static analysis of the abstract syntax
tree and dataflow localise testbench errors before simulation?
\item[\textnormal{\textbf{RQ3}}] Can a model informed by static analysis repair those errors
effectively, and how does this compare with compiler or simulator feedback alone?
\item[\textnormal{\textbf{RQ4}}] What is the cost--quality trade-off of static analysis
against feedback that requires compilation or simulation?
\end{description}

\section{Summary of Findings}\label{sec:intro-findings}

The answer to RQ2 has two halves that point in opposite directions, and stating both here is
preferable to letting a reader discover the second at page thirty.

The localiser works. Measured by injecting 215 faults of known identity into testbenches
known to pass, it detects 93\%, names the correct fault class and the correct signal in 93\%
of cases, and produces no findings at all on unmutated input. Thirty-three of those faults
are invisible to the compiler and the simulator together; the localiser catches 30 of them.

The localiser has almost nothing to find. Across 280 pipeline runs on two models and two
circuit sets, it reported findings on three runs in the 262 analyses in which Pyverilog could
build a syntax tree, and triggered one repair in the whole study, which cleared the finding
before a later regeneration undid it. The configuration whose sole purpose is to act on static findings performed
zero repairs in 44 runs. Meanwhile, in the 220-run ablation, 133 runs produced a testbench
that compiled and then failed simulation, which is 87\% of its failures.

Both follow from one fact: language models produce testbenches that are structurally well
formed and semantically wrong. They connect the ports and check the outputs. They misjudge
what the outputs should be.

Three alternative explanations were tested and rejected. A weaker model performs far worse at
the task and no worse structurally; substituting a stronger one on the harder circuits raises
mean Eval1 across the three shared arms from 10\% to 35\% and leaves the structural yield
unchanged. Twenty circuits from the
standard benchmark, selected for structural complexity before any run, produced one finding
under each generator. The localiser's correctness was established by measurement rather than
assumed.

\begin{enumerate}

\item A graph-based testbench generation pipeline with explicit routing, a bounded repair

\section{Simulation-Based Verification}\label{sec:bg-verification}

Functional verification asks whether a design does what it is supposed to do, and in
practice it is answered by simulation: drive the design with stimulus, observe its outputs,
compare them against what the specification requires. The program that performs this is the
testbench. It is ordinary Verilog, it is not synthesised into hardware, and its only purpose
is to exercise something else.

Writing testbenches is expensive. Surveys by the Wilson Research Group place roughly 49\% of
a design engineer's time on an IC or ASIC project in verification tasks~\cite{wilson2022}.
The work is also repetitive in a way that invites automation: for a large class of designs,
the testbench follows directly from the interface and the specification, and an engineer
writing one is transcribing rather than deciding.

\section{Structural and Semantic Testbench Faults}\label{sec:bg-fault-classes}

A testbench can be wrong in two ways, and the distinction runs through the whole of this
report.

A \textbf{structural} fault is one that can be identified by reading the testbench against
the design's interface. Listing~\ref{lst:structural} shows one. The testbench declares a
four-bit register and binds it to an eight-bit port. Nothing in the design's behaviour is
relevant to establishing that this is wrong; the widths disagree, and that is visible on the
page.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={A structural fault. The widths disagree, which is decidable from the text
alone without reference to the design's behaviour.},label={lst:structural},language=,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
reg [3:0] data_in;                    // 4 bits
fifo_8x8 uut (.data_in(data_in), ...) // port is 8 bits wide
\end{lstlisting}

A \textbf{semantic} fault is one that cannot. Listing~\ref{lst:semantic} shows a testbench
that is structurally impeccable: every port bound, every input driven, the output compared.
Whether \texttt{4'd1} is the right value depends entirely on how the counter behaves after a
reset, and that information is in the design, not in the testbench.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={A semantic fault. The code is well-formed; only the expected
value is wrong, and establishing that requires the design's behaviour.},
label={lst:semantic},language=,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
rst = 1; @(posedge clk); #1; rst = 0;
@(posedge clk); #1;
if (count === 4'd1) $display("PASS: after_reset");   // should be 4'd0
\end{lstlisting}

Static analysis can address the first class and cannot address the second at any level of
effort, because the information required is absent from its input. Which class dominates in
practice is an empirical question.

One asymmetry within the structural class deserves note here, since it motivates the design
in Chapter~\ref{ch:localiser}. A testbench that stops checking one of the design's outputs
is structurally faulty and \emph{passes} simulation, because a check that is not performed
cannot fail. This is the one case where static analysis is not merely faster than
simulation but is the only method available.

\section{Large Language Models for HDL}\label{sec:bg-llm-hdl}

Applying language models to hardware description languages has been explored from several
directions. Chip-Chat~\cite{blocklove2023chipchat} examined conversational co-design,
reporting a full processor taped out through an iterative dialogue with the model, and is
notable for documenting how much of the design intent had to be supplied by the engineer
rather than the model. ChipGPT~\cite{chang2023chipgpt} studied natural-language hardware
design more systematically. AutoChip~\cite{thakur2023autochip} closed a feedback loop by
returning compiler and simulator output to the model, which is the same mechanism as the
\texttt{compiler\_only} configuration in this work and, on the evidence of
Section~\ref{sec:res-rq4}, the most cost-effective of the ones tested.

Two efforts target verification rather than design. LLM4DV~\cite{zhang2023llm4dv} uses
models to generate test stimuli, and Orenes-Vera et
al.~\cite{orenes2023llmformal} apply them to formal verification of RTL. Both share the
observation that the generated artefact is frequently plausible and wrong, which is the
problem this project addresses from the static-analysis side.

VerilogEval~\cite{liu2023verilogeval} supplies the evaluation substrate the field has
converged on: 156 problems derived from HDLBits exercises, each with a natural-language
description, a reference design and a reference testbench. It is used here for the third
sweep (Section~\ref{sec:exp-circuits}).

\section{Static Analysis of Verilog}\label{sec:bg-static}

Pyverilog~\cite{takamaeda2015pyverilog} is a Python toolkit providing a Verilog parser, an
abstract syntax tree, and dataflow and control-flow analysis built on top of it. It is the
analysis backend used here, chosen because operating on an AST in Python allows checks to be
expressed against the structure of the design and the testbench together rather than against
text.

Its limitations are real. It targets Verilog-2001 and handles only part of SystemVerilog, it
was written for human-authored input, and it rejects constructs that appear in generated
code. Where it fails to parse, Verible~\cite{verible} is used as a fallback for a syntax
verdict. Section~\ref{sec:loc-parse} reports how often this was needed.

Simulation throughout uses Icarus Verilog~\cite{iverilog}, an open-source simulator
supporting IEEE 1364 and parts of IEEE 1800.

\section{Evaluation Criteria: Eval0, Eval1 and Eval2}\label{sec:bg-eval}

This work adopts the three-level evaluation introduced by
AutoBench~\cite{qiu2024autobench}, which allows results to be compared against theirs where
the comparison is meaningful.

\textbf{Eval0} asks whether the testbench compiles against the design. It is the weakest
criterion and a prerequisite for the other two.

\textbf{Eval1} asks whether the testbench passes when run against a design known to be
correct. A failure means the testbench expects something the reference design does not
produce, which locates the fault in the testbench.

\textbf{Eval2} asks whether the testbench detects faults. Mutants of the reference design
are produced by introducing a single bug, and the testbench is scored on how many it
catches. The criterion exists because Eval1 can be passed by a testbench that checks
nothing, and mutation testing~\cite{jia2011mutation} is the established way of exposing
that.

Eval2's value depends entirely on mutant difficulty, which is a recurring weakness of the
method and one this work encountered directly: Section~\ref{sec:disc-threats} reports the
measure at ceiling and treats it as a limitation rather than a result.

\section{AutoBench}\label{sec:bg-autobench}

AutoBench~\cite{qiu2024autobench} is the direct predecessor of this work. It generates a
testbench in stages rather than in one call: classify the circuit as combinational or
sequential, extract a structured specification, list test scenarios, generate a Verilog
driver, then generate a Python checker that reads the driver's output. The pipeline in
Chapter~\ref{ch:design} follows this decomposition.

Three self-enhancement mechanisms sit on top. Scenario checking verifies in Python that each
named scenario appears in the generated driver, retrying up to three times. Auto-debug
returns compiler errors with line-numbered source to the model for one repair attempt. Code
standardisation is a script that inserts missing \texttt{\$fdisplay} statements for
sequential circuits.

Reported results, on 156 VerilogEval problems with GPT-4-turbo, are given in
Table~\ref{tab:autobench-results} at pass@1.

The ablation attributes roughly 8\% of Eval2 pass@1 to auto-debug and roughly 10\% to
scenario checking. Sequential Eval0 rises from 55.47\% to 97.33\%, which the authors credit
primarily to code standardisation.

Their Eval2 criterion is not a raw detection rate. A testbench passes Eval2 on a problem when
its verdicts \emph{agree with the golden testbench's} on at least 80\% of that problem's
mutants, and the reported 44.81\% is the share of all 156 problems clearing that bar. Two
consequences follow for any comparison. Agreement is more forgiving than detection, because a
mutant the golden testbench also misses counts as agreement rather than as a miss; and the
denominator is all problems, not only those that passed Eval1.

Two properties of the system matter for what follows. Nothing in AutoBench parses the
Verilog it produces: scenario checking is a text search, auto-debug delegates to the
compiler, and standardisation is a targeted insertion. There is no analysis of port
bindings, signal widths or dataflow. And the ablation compares each mechanism against a
version of the system with that mechanism disabled, which also removes the regeneration
attempts the mechanism triggers, so the reported gains cannot separate the value of the
diagnosis from the value of trying again. Section~\ref{sec:exp-control} describes the
control arm this work adds for that reason.

\section{Gap Addressed by this Work}\label{sec:bg-gap}

Prior work detects faults in generated testbenches by compiling them, running them, or
searching their text. None of it parses them.

That leaves a specific question unanswered. Structural faults exist that the compiler
accepts and the simulator cannot observe, of which an unchecked output is the clearest
example. Whether such faults occur often enough in LLM-generated testbenches to be worth
detecting has not been measured, and cannot be inferred from pass rates, because a pass rate
does not say which defect caused a failure.

This work builds the analyser that would detect them, measures what it can detect by
injecting faults of known identity, and then measures how often it detects anything at all
across 280 pipeline runs on two models and two circuit sets. The contribution is the pair of
measurements rather than the analyser: the second is only interpretable given the first.

\chapter{System Design}\label{ch:design}
% PHASE 1 · target 5.5 pp

The pipeline takes a natural-language circuit description and returns a Verilog testbench,
a Python checker, and a record of everything that happened on the way. This chapter
describes how it is put together and why it is put together that way. The static analysis
layer is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:localiser} and is treated here only as one node
among several.

\section{Why a Graph Rather than a Script}\label{sec:design-graph}

Multi-stage LLM pipelines are usually written as imperative scripts: call the model, parse
the reply, call it again, retry on failure. That works until the control flow acquires a
loop. Once a repair step can send execution back to an earlier stage, the conditions
governing that jump are scattered across the function that happens to contain them, and
answering a question such as ``under which circumstances does this configuration
regenerate the driver?'' means reading the whole script.

We built the pipeline as a LangGraph~\cite{langgraph} state machine instead. Every stage is
a node, every transition is an edge, and every conditional transition is a named routing
function that takes the graph state and returns the name of the next node. The routing
functions are small, pure, and testable in isolation; \texttt{should\_repair} decides
whether static findings warrant a repair, \texttt{should\_repair\_after\_eval} decides the
same for compiler and simulator failures, and \texttt{after\_repair} decides where a
repaired testbench re-enters the graph.

This has a practical consequence for the ablation study in
Chapter~\ref{ch:experiment}. The five feedback strategies differ only in what those three
routing functions return; the nodes themselves are identical across all of them. That the
arms are genuinely distinct is therefore a property that can be checked by evaluating the
routing functions over a matrix of states, rather than a property that has to be trusted.

State is a single typed dictionary of thirty-five fields covering inputs, per-stage
outputs, loop control, evaluation results and telemetry. Two fields use LangGraph reducers
rather than plain assignment. \texttt{llm\_calls} accumulates with \texttt{operator.add} so
that the parallel driver and checker branches can each append a log entry without
overwriting the other's, and \texttt{static\_findings} accumulates for the same reason
across repeated analysis passes. The distinction matters: the analysis node runs once per
repair iteration, and a plain assignment would leave only the last pass visible, which is
precisely the evidence needed to answer RQ2.

A second field deserves mention because it exists to correct a measurement error rather
than to implement a feature. \texttt{task\_id} carries the logical identity of a circuit,
separately from \texttt{module\_name}, which carries the Verilog module name. Every
VerilogEval problem defines its module as \texttt{RefModule}, so aggregating on
\texttt{module\_name} would have collapsed all 156 problems into a single row. The bug was
found before the benchmark sweeps rather than after, which was luck.

\section{Generation Stages}\label{sec:design-generation}

Generation follows AutoBench~\cite{qiu2024autobench} in decomposing the task rather than
asking for a testbench in one call, and departs from it in generating the design under test
as well.

\texttt{classify} labels the circuit combinational or sequential from the description
alone. The label controls one routing decision (whether \texttt{standardise} runs) and one
analysis decision (whether the sequential-only checks apply).

\texttt{gen\_dut} synthesises a design from the description. AutoBench withholds the DUT
from generation on the grounds that a faulty DUT would mislead the model; we generate one
because the realistic flow is description to design to testbench, and because a testbench
written with privileged access to the reference answer is not being tested honestly. The
golden design is supplied at evaluation time only, and the field \texttt{eval\_dut\_source}
records which design each run was scored against.

\texttt{extract\_spec} produces a structured JSON summary of ports, behaviour and timing,
and \texttt{gen\_scenarios} turns that into a list of named test scenarios. The two
generation nodes then run as parallel branches: \texttt{gen\_driver} writes the Verilog
testbench, \texttt{gen\_checker} writes a Python checker for its output.
\texttt{merge\_generation} is a no-op that exists solely as a fan-in barrier, so that the
routing decision after generation sees both branches complete rather than racing them.

Model assignment is by task rather than by stage: classification, scenario listing and
mutant generation use the cheaper model, while specification extraction, code generation
and repair use the stronger one. Section~\ref{sec:res-rq4} reports what this costs.

\section{Deterministic Standardisation}\label{sec:design-standardiser}

A sequential testbench must make the design's outputs observable and must toggle the clock.
AutoBench addresses the first with a code standardisation step and reports it as the
largest single contributor to their sequential compilation rate, which rose from 55.47\% to
97.33\%. Their implementation is partly LLM-driven.

Ours is not. \texttt{fdisplay\_inserter.py} is a Python pass that inserts a
\texttt{\$monitor} covering any design output the testbench never observes, and a clock
generator when a declared clock is present but never toggled. Inserting a monitor statement
is a mechanical edit with a single correct answer, and a language model is the wrong tool
for it: it is slower, it costs tokens, and it can decline to do the job in ways that are
tedious to detect.

Three properties make the pass safe to run unconditionally. It is idempotent, marking its
own output with \verb|// [standardised]| and short-circuiting if that marker is already
present. It is fail-safe, returning the input unchanged if anything goes wrong, on the
principle that a missing monitor is a worse outcome than a corrupted testbench. And it
never edits the design, only the testbench.

\section{The Repair Loop}\label{sec:design-repair}

Three sources can trigger a repair: static findings from the analysis layer, compiler
errors from Eval0, and failing scenarios from Eval1. Each is converted into a structured
error report, rendered into a repair prompt alongside the current testbench, and used to
regenerate the driver. The loop is bounded at three iterations.

Two mechanisms stop it wasting effort. Oscillation detection compares a signature of the
current error set against the previous one and halts when they match, on the reasoning that
a model which has already failed to fix an error given that description will not fix it on
a second identical attempt; the check also fires when the regenerated testbench is
byte-identical to its predecessor. Best-so-far retention addresses a subtler problem. The
repair node regenerates the entire testbench rather than patching it, so a later iteration
can be worse than an earlier one. Before this was added, a configuration that repaired more
could score below one that repaired less, which is not a property an ablation should have.
The pipeline now tracks the highest-scoring evaluated testbench and reports that one, while
routing decisions continue to use the current iteration's result.

The second regeneration node, \texttt{regenerate}, exists for the control arm described in
Section~\ref{sec:exp-control}. It draws one additional sample from \texttt{gen\_driver} and
passes it nothing at all: no error report, no static findings, no simulator output. It is
deliberately uninformed.

\section{Evaluation Harness}\label{sec:design-harness}

The harness runs the pipeline over a set of circuits crossed with a set of ablation modes,
writing one JSON record per run. Three guards protect a long unattended sweep.

A budget guard prints the run count implied by a selection and refuses to start if it
exceeds twenty-four runs without an explicit opt-in. This is deliberately low. The refusal
performs zero runs, so an over-broad selection costs nothing.

Two abort conditions distinguish failures that will clear from failures that will not. A
rate limit naming a daily token quota, and an exhausted account balance, both mean that no
later run in the sweep can succeed either; the harness stops immediately and aggregates
what completed, rather than failing every remaining pair one at a time with its own retries
and backoff. An ordinary transient failure does not abort: the run is recorded with status
\texttt{harness\_error} and the sweep continues. Getting this distinction wrong in either
direction is expensive, and the tests assert both behaviours by counting invocations.

\section{Engineering Practices}\label{sec:design-practices}

Prompts are Jinja templates in \texttt{prompts/}, never inline strings, so that they can be
diffed, reviewed, and frozen at a tagged commit before an evaluation run begins
(Section~\ref{sec:exp-freeze}).

Every model call is logged with its node, model, token counts, latency and sampling
temperature. The cost analysis in Section~\ref{sec:res-rq4} is assembled from these records
rather than estimated.

The test suite comprises 201 tests and spends no API tokens. Live-API tests exist but are
marked and skip automatically unless a key is present in the environment, which keeps the
default suite free to run as often as needed. Several of the defects reported in
Section~\ref{sec:loc-falsepos} were caught only because running the full suite was cheap
enough to do after every change.

\chapter{The Static Localiser}\label{ch:localiser}
% PHASE 1 · target 4.0 pp

\section{Design Principle and Scope}\label{sec:loc-principle}

The localiser reads a generated testbench together with the design it instantiates and
reports structural defects. Its scope is fixed by one rule: it detects only what reading
the code can establish. Whether a testbench expects the correct value from a counter after
reset depends on how that counter behaves, which is information the text does not contain.
Whether a testbench drives an input it declared is visible on the page.

That rule is a limitation by construction, and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} shows it to be a
severe one in practice. It is also what makes the layer cheap: analysis is deterministic,
requires no model call, and completes in milliseconds.

Parsing uses Pyverilog~\cite{takamaeda2015pyverilog} to build an abstract syntax tree for
the testbench and the design together. Where Pyverilog rejects the input,
Verible~\cite{verible} is used as a fallback for a basic syntax verdict.

Table~\ref{tab:checks} lists the checks. The final two columns state whether the same

report. The same reasoning applies to \texttt{missing\_fdisplay}, which is the
sequential-specific form of the same defect.

\texttt{clock\_never\_toggled} covers a gap between two other checks. A testbench
containing \texttt{initial clk = 0;} and nothing further has driven the clock as far as the
undriven-input check is concerned, so that check stays silent. The simulation compiles and
runs, but no clock edge ever occurs, the design never leaves its reset state, and every
scenario reads back the reset value. The failure presents as a logic error, which is an
expensive way to discover a missing clock generator.

\section{Width Mismatch: Silent at Simulation Time}\label{sec:loc-width}

Verilog resolves a width mismatch on a port connection by padding or truncating. It is not
an error and, in Icarus Verilog~\cite{iverilog}, not a failure either. Compiling a
testbench that declares \verb|wire [2:0] out| against a design whose port is four bits
produces:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
tb.v:3: warning: Port 3 (out) of module counter_4bit expects 4 bit(s), given 3.
tb.v:3:        : Padding 1 high bits of the port.
\end{lstlisting}

The compiler exits with status zero. The testbench builds, runs, and silently compares the
wrong values. Catching this by simulation is possible but unreliable, since it depends on
whether the scenarios happen to exercise the truncated bits; Section~\ref{sec:val-results}
measures how often they do. The check itself compares each design port's declared width
against the testbench signal bound to it, reading both ANSI and Verilog-1995 declaration
styles, and produces no finding for widths it cannot resolve, such as those given by a
parameter or an expression. Guessing would be worse than silence here.

\section{A Check that was Removed}\label{sec:loc-removed}

A seventh check, \texttt{sensitivity\_list\_error}, was implemented and later deleted. It
looked for \texttt{always} blocks inside the testbench and reported an error when none of
them used \texttt{posedge} or \texttt{negedge} sensitivity, on the reasoning that a
testbench driving a sequential design ought to synchronise to the clock.

The fault-injection study in Chapter~\ref{ch:validation} scored it at zero: of five injected
faults that removed edge synchronisation from a testbench --- the concern the check was
written to address --- it detected none, and it had produced the study's only baseline false
positive.

The reason is structural, and it is why those five faults are classified as a negative
control in Section~\ref{sec:val-injectors} rather than as a class the method failed on.
Generated testbenches drive the design from \texttt{initial} blocks and synchronise using
\texttt{@(posedge clk)} event controls, not from edge-triggered \texttt{always} blocks. The
only \texttt{always} block such a testbench typically contains is its clock generator, which
has no sensitivity list by design. Replacing an event control with a bare delay leaves a
testbench that is structurally identical to a correct one; the defect is in when it samples,
which is behaviour, not structure. The check was inspecting a construct the code does not
use, in pursuit of a defect no structural check could have found.

We removed it rather than repair it. A check with no measured recall and a history of false
positives is worse than no check, because it consumes the repair budget on nothing. The
gap it leaves is recorded in the test suite so that it is not rediscovered later as a bug:
a testbench that drives a sequential design entirely with bare delays is not reported, and
the simulator catches four in five of those.

\section{False Positives as the Primary Risk}\label{sec:loc-falsepos}

A localiser that misses a defect costs one simulation cycle. A localiser that invents one
costs a model call, a regeneration, and the credibility of every finding it subsequently
reports. Four false-positive defects were found during development, all of the same
character: a check that encoded one particular way of writing a testbench and flagged every
other correct way.

The most consequential was not a false positive but its mirror image, and it suppressed
detection outright. Several checks ask questions of the source text, such as whether a
signal is ever compared or printed. Asked of raw source, comments and string literals can
answer those questions with text that is not code:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
$display("PASS: addition_boundary_overflow");   // the word "overflow" appears here
$fdisplay(f, "clk=%b out=%b", clk, out);        // so does an apparent assignment to clk
\end{lstlisting}

The first line made the \texttt{overflow} output of an ALU appear to be observed when the
testbench had stopped checking it. The second made a clock that was assigned once and never
toggled appear to be toggling. Both are false negatives that hide real defects, and the
same class of error was independently found in the Eval1 verdict, where a scenario named
\texttt{immediate\_mismatch} caused its own run to be scored as a failure.

The fix is a preparation pass that blanks the contents of comments and string literals
while preserving length and line breaks, so that callers matching on the prepared text can
still edit the original at the same offsets. Detection for the unobserved-output class
moved from 47\% to 100\% once it was applied.

We report these defects because they bear on how the results should be read. The figures in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} were produced by a localiser whose measured behaviour moved by
53 percentage points on one fault class during development. The fault-injection study
exists partly because nothing in the ordinary test suite had revealed any of this.

\section{Parse Robustness on Generated Code}\label{sec:loc-parse}

Pyverilog is documented as a research tool and was not written for machine-generated input,
and this turned out to be the binding constraint on the whole layer. Across the 434 analyses
of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, Pyverilog built an abstract syntax tree for 262. In 168 it
failed and the pipeline fell back to Verible; in 4 both failed.

The distinction matters more than a parse-success rate suggests, because the fallback is not
an equivalent analysis. Verible is invoked for a syntax verdict only: it returns a report
that is well-formed and empty, and none of the six checks run. Those 168 analyses are
recorded as \texttt{parse\_ok} and contribute nothing to the question RQ2 asks.

layer returned an empty report, and an empty report is indistinguishable from a clean one.
The static-analysis arm of the ablation was silently identical to the baseline for an entire
round of experiments before this was found. The fallback reintroduces the same ambiguity in
a legitimate form: a clean report and an unanalysed file are the same object, and only the
recorded parser name separates them. Section~\ref{sec:res-rq2b} therefore reports the yield
against the analyses that were actually parsed rather than against every analysis attempted.

\chapter{Validating the Localiser}\label{ch:validation}
% PHASE 2 · target 4.0 pp

\section{Why Pass Rates Cannot Answer RQ2}\label{sec:val-motivation}

RQ2 asks how well static analysis narrows down testbench errors before simulation. The
obvious way to answer it is to run the pipeline with the static layer enabled and disabled
and compare pass rates. That answer would be worthless, for two reasons.

A pass rate is a single bit per run. When a testbench fails, the rate does not say which
defect caused it, and therefore cannot say whether the analyser could have caught that
defect. When a testbench passes, the rate says nothing about whether the analyser would
have caught a defect that was never there. Neither outcome constrains the quantity RQ2 asks
about.

The second reason is variance. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports a measured spread of 33
percentage points between configurations that execute identical code at twelve circuits per
arm, which is larger than any effect the comparison is trying to detect. A difference in pass rates at this sample
size would not be evidence either way.

So we measured the analyser directly, by giving it faults whose identity we already knew.

\section{Fault Injection Method}\label{sec:val-method}

Take a testbench already known to pass. Break it in one known way. Ask three layers the
same question:

\begin{itemize}
  \setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
  \item does the static analyser report a finding?
  \item does \texttt{iverilog} refuse to compile it?
  \item does the simulation fail against the golden design?
\end{itemize}

The three-way comparison is what makes the result interpretable. A fault only the analyser
catches is a fault the existing toolchain cannot find at all, and is the strongest possible
case for the layer. A fault the compiler already catches is one the layer adds nothing to
beyond speed. Reporting the first without the second would be selective.

The corpus is 14 testbenches, one per circuit, taken from recorded runs that passed Eval1.
The fourteen circuits are the twelve of the project set (Section~\ref{sec:exp-circuits})
together with \texttt{half\_adder} and \texttt{mux2to1}, two early development fixtures that
were not carried into the sweeps but had passing testbenches on record. These are real
generated artefacts rather than hand-written examples. That distinction matters, because a
hand-written testbench would carry the author's assumptions about what a testbench looks
like, and those assumptions are exactly what the checks encode. Measuring a checker against
inputs shaped by the same beliefs that produced it would flatter it. Injecting into all 14
produced 215 faults.

One property of the corpus limits what the headline figures establish, and it is stated here
rather than in the threats section because it bears on how Table~\ref{tab:injection} should
be read. There is no held-out split. The same 14 testbenches that produced the study's
figures also produced the defects the study found: the comment-and-string-literal bug whose
repair moved one class from 47\% to 100\%, the single false positive, and the evidence on
which \texttt{sensitivity\_list\_error} was deleted. Detection was therefore tuned and
measured on one corpus. The 93\% is an in-sample figure, and a fresh set of circuits would be
required to turn it into a predictive one.

\section{Injectors and Negative Controls}\label{sec:val-injectors}

Eight injectors cover the taxonomy. Six target a check directly: renaming a port in the
instantiation, dropping a port connection, narrowing a declared signal width, redirecting
an input's assignments so the input is never driven, removing an output from the
testbench's comparisons, and deleting the clock generator.

Two are negative controls, and they are the more interesting half. \texttt{break\_edge\_sync}
replaces every \texttt{@(posedge clk)} with a bare delay, and \texttt{swap\_bindings} binds
two same-width inputs to each other's signals. Neither is detectable by any structural
check. The second in particular produces a testbench in which every connection is legal and
correctly typed, and only the meaning is wrong.

Including faults the method provably cannot catch is a deliberate choice. A study
containing only detectable faults reports a detection rate that describes the study design
rather than the method. The controls mark where the boundary actually falls, and
Section~\ref{sec:val-limits} states what fell outside it.

\section{Methodological Safeguards}\label{sec:val-safeguards}

Three properties of the harness were necessary to make the comparison mean anything. Each
was added after the corresponding mistake was made.

\textbf{Mutations must produce legal Verilog.} An injector that emits a syntax error would
score as ``the compiler caught it'' for a defect the injector itself introduced. The
undriven-input injector therefore redirects assignments to a fresh register rather than
deleting statements, because deleting a statement can empty an \texttt{if} branch and
produce exactly that artefact. Injectors that cannot mutate a given testbench cleanly
decline, and the declined cases are excluded from the denominator rather than counted as
misses.

\textbf{The corpus must use golden designs, not generated ones.} A recorded run stores the
design the pipeline generated, which can itself be malformed. An early version of the study
compiled mutated testbenches against a generated design that did not build, and scored that
design's defect as a detection of the injected fault. The corpus now loads the reference
design from the fixture set.

\textbf{Every corpus entry is re-verified before injection.} The unmutated testbench must
compile and its simulation must pass against the golden design. Measuring detection against
a baseline that is already failing attributes the baseline's problems to the injected
fault. Entries that fail this gate are excluded and reported.

\section{The Case Only Static Analysis Detects}\label{sec:val-unobserved}

The first row of Table~\ref{tab:injection} carries more weight than the rest. Nineteen
faults were injected by removing an output from the testbench's checks; the analyser caught
all nineteen, the compiler none, the simulator none.

The simulator's score is not a shortcoming of the simulator. A testbench that stops checking
an output does not fail on account of that output. It passes, because it is no longer
looking, and it will keep passing however many times it is run. There is no observation the
simulator could make that would distinguish this testbench from a correct one. The compiler
sees legal Verilog throughout.

This is the clearest form of the argument for pre-simulation analysis, and it is worth
stating plainly before Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports how rarely the situation arises in
practice.

\section{Boundaries of the Method}\label{sec:val-limits}

Binding a port under a name the design does not have is detected by the analyser and by the
compiler equally, at 100\% each. Sixty-two of the 215 faults fall in this class, so a
substantial part of the headline detection rate describes work the existing toolchain
already does. What the analyser contributes there is speed, not capability.

The two control classes were detected at zero, as designed. Swapping two same-width inputs
leaves a testbench in which every port is bound, every signal is driven, every output is
checked, and every width agrees. The defect is in what the testbench means, not in how it is
built. The simulator caught 78\% of those, which is the correct division of labour.

\paragraph{The boundary is narrower than ``by construction'' suggests.} That phrasing is
accurate for the six checks as implemented and too strong as a statement about structural
analysis in general. Two candidate checks, written after the study and measured against the
same corpus, reach both control classes; Table~\ref{tab:candidate-checks} gives their scores.

The first is the deleted \texttt{sensitivity\_list\_error} check
(Section~\ref{sec:loc-removed}) looking in the right place. It failed originally because it

blocks, so the same concern expressed over event controls rather than block headers recovers
the full class.

The second exploits a convention rather than a semantic property, and that limits it.
Generated testbenches bind ports to identically-named signals almost without exception --- 7
of 7 and 9 of 9 in the fixtures inspected --- so exchanging two bindings breaks the convention
visibly. A testbench naming its signals \texttt{sig\_a} and \texttt{sig\_b} would swap them
undetectably. It is a heuristic with measured precision on this population, not a proof.

Both are reported as measured possibilities rather than implemented checks: neither was part
of the frozen analyser, and adding a check after seeing which faults it must catch is exactly
the in-sample fitting Section~\ref{sec:val-method} warns about. What they establish is that
the boundary of the method was drawn by the checks that happened to be written, and is
movable.

\paragraph{Moving it would buy nothing.} The boundary being movable and the boundary being
worth moving are different questions, and the second is answerable from the data already
collected. Both candidate checks were run over the stored testbench of every one of the 280
pipeline runs.

The binding-name check fired \textbf{zero times in 280 runs}. Exchanged bindings, the fault it
exists to catch, do not occur in this corpus at all.

The clock-edge check fired three times, all three on \texttt{Prob150\_\allowbreak
review2015\_\allowbreak fsmonehot} under different configurations --- and all three are false
positives. That problem is a one-hot next-state decoder: it is combinational, and neither the
reference design nor the generated one contains a clock or an edge-sensitive construct. A
testbench for it is \emph{correct} to contain no clock-edge wait. The check fired because the
classification stage had labelled the circuit sequential, wrongly, in 1 of the 16 sequential
labels it assigned across the benchmark sweeps.

That failure is worth more than the check. A structural check inherits the errors of whatever
stage supplies its context; this one would have converted a single upstream misclassification
into three confident and incorrect findings, in a layer whose entire credibility rests on
having produced no false positives (Section~\ref{sec:loc-falsepos}). The two classes excluded
by the negative controls are therefore reachable in principle, absent from real output in
practice, and costly to pursue --- which is the same conclusion the six implemented checks
reach, one level further down.

\section{Circuit Sets and Selection Criteria}\label{sec:exp-circuits}

The project set is twelve circuits in two halves. Six are small and were used during
development: two-bit comparators, a D flip-flop, a four-bit counter. Six were written later
and deliberately made harder, with more ports and mixed signal widths: an eight-bit ALU with
carry and overflow flags, a barrel shifter, a nine-port FIFO, an overlapping sequence
detector, a timed traffic-light controller. The second group exists because four of the six
static checks cannot fire on an eight-line circuit with two unambiguous ports, and measuring
a checker on inputs that cannot trigger it is not a measurement.

The third sweep uses VerilogEval~\cite{liu2023verilogeval}, which is the benchmark AutoBench
evaluated on. Running all 156 problems was outside the project's budget, so twenty were
selected. How they were selected determines whether the result is usable, so the procedure
is stated in full.

Every problem was scored before any run took place, using three properties of its reference
design: the number of ports, the number of distinct signal widths, and whether it contains
edge-triggered logic. The twenty highest-scoring problems were taken. Their scores range
from 26.4 to 41.9 against a median of 18.7 across all 156, port counts run from six to
twelve, and fifteen of the twenty are sequential.

The criterion is structural complexity because structural faults are only possible where
structure is complex. This biases the sample \emph{towards} the conditions in which the
localiser can succeed, and that is the point. A null result obtained under favourable
conditions is stronger than one obtained under arbitrary conditions.

The alternative procedure, running all 156 and reporting the twenty that produced findings,
would have selected on the outcome. It would have produced a better-looking number that
meant nothing, and it would not have survived the first question about how the circuits were
chosen.

\section{The No-Diagnostics Control}\label{sec:exp-control}

Every repairing configuration receives something the baseline does not: a second generation.
If \texttt{hybrid} outperforms \texttt{baseline}, two explanations fit equally well. The
feedback may have identified the defect and guided the repair. Or the model may simply have
had a second attempt, and sampling twice from a stochastic generator produces a better
result often enough to explain the gap without any feedback being involved.

\texttt{retry\_only} separates them. It draws exactly one additional sample from
\texttt{gen\_driver} and passes it no error report, no static findings and no simulator
output. Any configuration must beat \texttt{retry\_only}, not merely \texttt{baseline},
before its feedback can be credited with the improvement.

AutoBench's ablation has no equivalent arm. Its reported gains of roughly 8\% from
auto-debug and 10\% from scenario checking are measured against versions of the system with
those mechanisms disabled, which also removes the regeneration attempts they trigger. The
comparison therefore cannot distinguish the two explanations above.
Section~\ref{sec:res-rq3} reports what happens when the control is present.

\section{Prompt Freezing}\label{sec:exp-freeze}

All nine prompt templates were frozen at a tagged commit before the first sweep and were not
modified afterwards. The tag records a checksum of the template set so the claim can be
checked rather than taken on trust.

The protection this offers is against a specific and easy mistake: observing a poor result,
adjusting a prompt, re-running, and reporting the improved number as though the prompt had
been fixed in advance. One caveat has to be stated alongside it. The driver prompt was tuned
during development against the six original project fixtures, so those six are not held out.
The six purpose-built circuits and all twenty VerilogEval problems were never used for
prompt tuning.

\section{Statistical Treatment}\label{sec:exp-stats}

Pass rates are proportions from small samples, and the usual normal approximation behaves
badly at the extremes. Confidence intervals are therefore Wilson score
intervals~\cite{wilson1927}, which remain sensible when a proportion approaches zero or one.

Comparisons between arms use Fisher's exact test~\cite{fisher1922} rather than a chi-square
test. The reason is not low cell counts: with 44 runs per arm and pass counts between nine
and eighteen, the smallest expected frequency in any of the pairwise tables is 10.5, well
above the conventional threshold of five. Fisher's test is used because it is exact and
makes no asymptotic assumption, so one test can be applied uniformly across every comparison
rather than switching tests according to how the cells happen to fall.

Fisher's test treats the two arms as independent samples, which understates the information
available here. Every circuit is run in all five configurations, so the arms are matched.
Comparisons are therefore also reported as McNemar exact tests on the discordant pairs, which
use the pairing and are the more powerful test. Where the two disagree, both are given.
Ten pairwise comparisons between arms are computed, so an unadjusted $p<0.05$ on any single
one is weak evidence; the Bonferroni-corrected threshold within that set is $p<0.005$. Those
ten are part of a larger family --- at least twenty tests are reported in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} --- and Section~\ref{sec:res-strata} applies the wider correction
where it matters.

All are computed by \texttt{scripts/analyse\_results.py}, which reads the same JSON records
the pipeline writes. No figure in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} was transcribed by hand.

The 133 failing runs contain 569 individual failing scenarios. Grouping them by keyword in

What the categories share is more informative than how they divide. Failures such as
\texttt{right\_arithmetic\_shift\_2\_positions}, \texttt{read\_from\_full\_fifo} and

behaves in a corner case. Determining that any of them is wrong requires knowing the
reference behaviour, which the testbench text does not contain.

\subsection*{Is the testbench wrong, or the design it was written for?}

That reading has an alternative that has to be ruled out. The pipeline generates its own
design (Section~\ref{sec:design-generation}) and the testbench is written against it, but
Eval1 scores the testbench against the reference design. A testbench that correctly
implements the behaviour of an \emph{incorrect} generated design would fail Eval1 without the
model having misjudged anything, and would be counted above as a semantic testbench fault.

The 133 failing testbenches were therefore re-simulated against the generated designs they
were actually written for. If a testbench passes there, its expectations are self-consistent
and the Eval1 failure belongs to the design; if it fails there too, the testbench contradicts
its own design and the fault is the testbench's. Table~\ref{tab:dut-attrib} gives the split.

figure should be read as 108 confirmed, 22 indeterminate and 3 attributed to the

the reconstructed pre-standardisation text of those three runs produces no findings. The
ordering therefore accounts for none of the null result.

The \texttt{pyverilog\_only} configuration performed zero repairs in all three sweeps. It
is the arm whose entire purpose is to act on static findings, and across 44 runs it never
had one to act on. Its behaviour was therefore identical to \texttt{baseline} throughout,
a fact that Section~\ref{sec:res-variance} makes use of.

The single finding in the benchmark sweep deserves a note. It occurred on

port-dense circuit in the selection, and it landed in the \texttt{compiler\_only}
configuration, which ignores static findings by design. It was detected and not acted upon.
The same circuit produced structurally clean testbenches in the other four configurations,
so the defect was a random generation slip rather than a property of the circuit. It recurs
in Section~\ref{sec:res-strong}, where the same circuit is the only one in a fourth sweep to
produce a finding.

each record a single uninformed regeneration and no diagnosis. Five of those 15 repairs
produced a passing testbench, which Section~\ref{sec:res-repair-fail} compares against the
weaker model.

feedback does nothing; it is an absence of evidence that it does something, and the next two
sections explain why that absence was difficult to avoid: the repair mechanism succeeds
rarely, and the measurement floor is high.

\section{The Two Samples Together}\label{sec:res-strata}

The comparison this project depends on --- \texttt{hybrid} against the no-diagnostics control
--- now exists in two independent samples: the 44 matched circuits of the ablation, and the 20
of the benchmark sweep. They use different generators and different circuits, and as
Table~\ref{tab:strata} shows, they agree.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l r r r r@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Sample} & \textbf{matched} & \textbf{hybrid only} & \textbf{control only} & \textbf{McNemar} \\

Pooling matched pairs across strata is defensible when the effect points the same way in each,
and here it does, 6:1 and 5:1, before any pooling is done. Eleven circuits pass under
\texttt{hybrid} and fail under the control; two go the other way.

Three qualifications have to travel with that $p=0.022$, and they are not decoration.

It is a post-hoc analysis. The benchmark sweep was not planned as part of the ablation. It was
run to check the null result against a stronger generator, \texttt{hybrid} was observed to
lead, the control arm was then added, and the two samples were pooled afterwards. That
sequence is the one this report criticises elsewhere, and naming it is the minimum owed to a
reader.

It does not survive correction. This is one of at least twenty tests reported in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}; a Bonferroni threshold across that family is $p<0.0025$.

The strata are not exchangeable. They differ in generation model and in circuit set, so a
common underlying effect is an assumption rather than an observation.

What can be said, then, is narrower than the p-value suggests and stronger than either sample
alone. Across 64 circuit-matched pairs and two generators, informed repair beat uninformed
regeneration on 11 circuits and lost on 2; the direction is consistent and the magnitude is
similar in both samples; and a stratified test returns $p=0.022$, which is suggestive and
uncorrected. This work does not claim the effect is established. It claims the evidence now
points one way rather than none, which is a change from the position in
Section~\ref{sec:res-rq3}.

The companion result is cleaner and needs no such hedging. \texttt{retry\_only} beat
\texttt{baseline} in neither sample --- $p=1.000$ in both, 5:4 and 2:1 discordant. An extra
generation attempt carrying no diagnosis is worth approximately nothing on either circuit set.
Since \texttt{hybrid} and \texttt{retry\_only} both receive that attempt, whatever separates
them is the diagnosis and not the resampling.

Stating it this way is more informative than the pass rate alone, and it explains the

succeeds on 3 cannot move a 44-run arm detectably.

\section{Why Repairs Fail}\label{sec:res-repair-fail}

The 20 unsuccessful repairs divide into two failure modes, and the error signatures recorded
at each iteration separate them cleanly.

\paragraph{The diagnosis is not acted on (7 runs).} These terminated as
\texttt{oscillated} after a single repair iteration, which is the loop detecting that the
regenerated testbench failed the identical set of scenarios as its predecessor. The model was
given the failing scenario names and the simulator's output, rewrote the testbench, and
produced one that fails in exactly the same place.

\paragraph{Each repair relocates the failure (13 runs).} These exhausted the three-iteration
budget. Their error signatures changed at \emph{every} iteration: across the 14 runs that
reached a second diagnosed iteration, not one repeated a signature. The model does address
the failure it is shown, and in doing so produces a different one. The loop does not
converge; it runs out of budget.

The second mode is the more informative, because it bounds what a larger iteration budget
would buy. A loop that repeated a signature would be stuck and could be cut short, which is
what oscillation detection is for. A loop that produces a new failure every time is not
stuck --- it is exchanging one corner case for another, and there is no evidence in these
records that a fourth or fifth iteration would terminate.

Three configurations performed no repairs at all in the first two sweeps:
\texttt{baseline} by definition, \texttt{compiler\_only} because every testbench compiled,
and \texttt{pyverilog\_only} because no static finding ever occurred. Having taken the same
path through the graph, they differ only in the random draw from the model, and
Table~\ref{tab:variance} reports how far apart that draw put them.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l c c c r@{}}

Thirty-three points, twice, on different models and independently. Six of the twelve
circuits in the first sweep produced different pass/fail outcomes across arms that ran the
same code.

The floor this implies is specific to the sample size at which it was measured, and it does
not transfer to the pooled comparison unchanged. At twelve circuits per arm and a pass rate
near 0.4, the standard deviation of a single arm is 14 points, and the expected range across
three identical arms is 24 points; the observed 33 is a high but unremarkable draw rather
than a constant. At the 44 circuits of Section~\ref{sec:res-rq3} the same calculation gives
7.4 points for one arm and an expected range of 12 points across three. Quoting 33 points
against a pooled comparison would overstate the floor by roughly a factor of three.

The pooled figure has a direct empirical check, because two of the pooled arms also execute
identical logic: \texttt{baseline} and \texttt{pyverilog\_only} differ by 6.8 points over
the full 44 runs, with nine discordant circuits split six to three. That 6.8 points is a
measured null gap at exactly the sample size the ablation is read at, and it is the number
the eleven-point hybrid--control gap should be judged against, not the 33.

Read that way the gap is roughly 1.7 times the measured null rather than a third of it, which
is why it ranks first and still fails to reach significance. Resolving it would
require either a much larger circuit set or enough repeats to average the variance down, and
the budget allowed neither.

We report this as a result rather than as a caveat. It bears directly on how work in this
area is evaluated: AutoBench reports single-run pass@1 comparisons without error bars, and
at the variance measured here such comparisons would not support the conclusions drawn from
them.

model-dependent results in Sections~\ref{sec:res-repair-fail} and~\ref{sec:res-strong}.

That is a cross-arm difference of 25 points, and Section~\ref{sec:res-variance} has just
argued that differences of that order at this sample size need to be treated carefully. The
claim rests instead on the paired form of the same comparison, which is available because
every circuit is run in both arms. On those twenty circuits, five compiled under
\texttt{baseline} and failed to compile under \texttt{retry\_only}; none went the other way.

entirely one-directional is the substance of the finding: with that generator, uninformed
regeneration never recovered a circuit and lost five.

One thing this evidence is not, and an earlier draft of this report claimed it was: it is not
a within-run measurement. The control arm regenerates before evaluation, so the sample it
discards is never compiled, and no record exists of whether it would have. The comparison is
between arms on matched circuits, which is weaker, because the two arms draw independent
first samples.

A second attempt is not free. Where the first attempt was adequate, discarding it and
sampling again risks a worse draw, and on harder circuits that risk is realised often
enough to be visible. Any repair mechanism that regenerates rather than patches inherits
this exposure, which is the reason the pipeline retains the best-scoring testbench rather
than the most recent one.

\section{Consistency Across Models and Circuit Sets}\label{sec:res-consistency}

The 42 simulation-triggered and 8 compile-triggered repair \emph{iterations} across the
three sweeps account for every repair the pipeline performed on the basis of a diagnosis.
They are distributed over the 23 \emph{runs} of Table~\ref{tab:repair-success}, since a run
may repair up to three times. The remaining 44 repair events were the control arm's
uninformed regenerations, one per run. No repair in the three ablation sweeps was triggered
by a static finding; the single instance in the study occurred in the fourth
(Section~\ref{sec:res-strong}) and did not succeed.

Absolute pass rates vary between sweeps by a wide margin, as intended: the benchmark
circuits are much harder, with \texttt{baseline} Eval1 falling from 67\% on the project
fixtures to 10\% on VerilogEval with the weaker generator and 25\% with the stronger one. The
qualitative picture does not vary across any of the four. Static findings remain at or near
zero under both generators and both circuit sets, \texttt{pyverilog\_only} never repairs, and
simulation feedback drives whatever improvement occurs. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} takes up
what follows from that.

\section{Eval2 and the Quality of the Mutants}\label{sec:res-eval2}

Eval2 sits at 324 of 335 mutants caught, 97\%, and discriminates between no two
configurations. A measure at ceiling is either reporting that the testbenches are excellent
or reporting that the instrument is blunt, and the two cannot be told apart from the number
itself. This section separates them, because the answer turns out to be neither of the
obvious ones.

\paragraph{The mutant generator was defective.} Mutants were produced by asking the cheaper
model, in five independent calls, for one subtle bug each. With no shared context between
calls the model returns its most obvious candidate every time. For the \texttt{dff} fixture
all five mutants were the same edit, \texttt{q <= \textasciitilde d}. Five mutants, one
defect.

\paragraph{Repairing it does not move the result.} A controlled pilot regenerated the
mutants under AutoBench's own prompt, which requests all ten in a single response and
instructs the model to spread the edits over different positions and modification types. The
criteria for accepting a generator were fixed in advance and concern mutant quality only:
validity of at least 80\%, positional diversity, and an unmodified module header. The

\chapter{Discussion}\label{ch:discussion}
% PHASE 4 · target 5.5 pp

The central result is that a localiser which detects 93\% of injected faults found defects
on three runs in 262 analyses of real generated testbenches. A null result of that kind is only
worth reading if the obvious explanations for it have been excluded, and excluding them is
what most of this chapter does.

\section{Excluding the Alternative Explanations}\label{sec:disc-alternatives}

Five explanations would account for the result without saying anything about static
analysis. Four were anticipated and tested. The fifth was identified after the sweeps had
run, is not excluded, and is stated last.

\paragraph{The generation model was too capable.} The first sweep used
\texttt{claude-sonnet-4.5}, a strong model, and it is reasonable to suppose that a weaker
one would make the structural mistakes the checks look for. The second sweep tested this
directly by holding the circuits fixed and substituting \texttt{gpt-4o-mini}.

The weaker model is worse at the task by a wide margin. Baseline Eval1 fell from 67\% to
17\% on identical circuits. It is not worse in the way the explanation requires: static
findings went from 0 in 75 parsed analyses to 1 in 51. The additional failures were semantic, and
the structural quality of its testbenches was indistinguishable from the stronger model's.

The fourth sweep tests the same explanation from the opposite direction, and this matters
more, because until it was run the stronger model had never been applied to the benchmark
circuits. Substituting \texttt{claude-sonnet-4.5} for \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} on those twenty
problems raised \texttt{baseline} Eval1 from 10\% to 25\% and \texttt{hybrid} from 10\% to
50\%, with no change to the pipeline, the prompts or the circuits. The structural yield did
not follow: one run with findings in 120 analyses, both records
\texttt{port\_binding\_mismatch}, every other check silent.

Both directions therefore agree. Capability determines whether a testbench is right; it does
not determine whether it is well formed. Twenty-five points of Eval1 moved and the structural
yield did not.

\paragraph{The circuits were too simple.} Four of the six checks cannot fire on a circuit
with two unambiguous ports, and the twelve project fixtures were chosen by us. The third
sweep used twenty problems from VerilogEval, the benchmark AutoBench evaluated on, selected
by structural complexity before any run took place. Fifteen of the twenty are sequential,
port counts run to twelve, and complexity scores sit well above the benchmark median.

One finding, in the 64 of 153 analyses Pyverilog could parse. The sample was deliberately
biased towards the conditions in which the localiser can succeed and the result did not move.
The biasing was partly self-defeating: the same structural complexity that makes a circuit a
good test also makes its testbench more likely to use constructs the parser rejects, which is
why this sweep has the worst parse coverage of the three.

\paragraph{The localiser was broken.} It had been, twice, and both defects were found
before the sweeps rather than after. Chapter~\ref{ch:validation} exists to answer this
objection with a measurement rather than an assurance: 93\% detection, 93\% localisation,
no findings on 14 clean testbenches. The analysis was additionally re-run independently over
the swept testbenches after the sweeps completed, and reproduced the stored results exactly.
The instrument was working when it reported nothing.

\paragraph{The wrong faults were chosen.} The six checks were not selected arbitrarily. They
cover the defect classes prior work found worth correcting, and in one case exactly so:
AutoBench's code standardisation step inserts missing \texttt{\$fdisplay} statements, which
is the \texttt{missing\_fdisplay} class in Table~\ref{tab:checks}. That check fired zero
times. Section~\ref{sec:disc-autobench} takes up what changed.

\paragraph{The testbench was analysed against a design it could not disagree with.} This one
is not excluded. The localiser reads the testbench together with the design the pipeline
generated in the same run from the same specification
(Section~\ref{sec:loc-principle}); evaluation, in all 280 runs, compiles the testbench
against the supplied golden design instead (Section~\ref{sec:design-harness}). Every one of
the six checks tests agreement between two artefacts, and in deployment those two artefacts
were co-generated: the same model wrote both interfaces from the same port list, moments
apart. Agreement produced that way is not the same property as agreement between a testbench
and an independently supplied design, and it is the latter that the injection study measured
--- Section~\ref{sec:val-method} records that the corpus was deliberately built on reference
designs rather than generated ones. A mechanism of this shape would produce the observed null
whether or not structural faults have stopped occurring, and the study cannot separate the
two. The single static-triggered repair of Section~\ref{sec:res-strong} is the one place
where the two designs visibly diverged, and there the localiser's finding was correct about
the generated design and wrong about the design the testbench was graded against.

The null result is therefore established for the co-generation flow and does not transfer to
a flow in which the design is given. Re-running the frozen localiser over every stored
testbench paired with its golden design would settle it, costs no model calls, and is the
first item of Section~\ref{sec:concl-future}.

\section{Why Generated Testbenches Are Structurally Sound}\label{sec:disc-why}

A Verilog testbench has a highly regular shape. Declare registers for the inputs and wires
for the outputs, instantiate the design and bind every port, generate a clock if one is
needed, drive stimulus, compare against expected values. That shape is repeated across every
testbench in a training corpus, and reproducing it is the kind of task language models are
reliably good at.

Deciding what the expected value \emph{is} requires something else. It requires holding the
circuit's behaviour in mind and working out what a four-bit counter reads after three clock
edges with reset asserted midway, or what a FIFO reports when read and written in the same
cycle. That is not pattern reproduction, and the failures in Section~\ref{sec:res-rq1} are
concentrated exactly there: 95 of 569 failing scenarios concern sequence or state behaviour,
72 sit at boundary conditions.

The distinction matters for tooling. Structural correctness is cheap for a model to achieve
and cheap for a static analyser to verify, which means the two are competing for the same
easy ground. Semantic correctness is expensive for the model and unavailable to static
analysis at any price, because the information needed to check it is not in the text.

\section{The Same Technique in a Different Model Generation}\label{sec:disc-autobench}

AutoBench reports that its code standardisation step raised sequential compilation from
55.47\% to 97.33\%, the largest single gain in the paper. That step is a mechanical
pre-simulation fix for missing output observation, and it belongs to the same family as the
work reported here.

It succeeded because GPT-4-turbo omitted \texttt{\$fdisplay} often enough for a script that
inserted it to be worth 42 percentage points. Two years later the equivalent check found
nothing to insert across 262 analyses, and the standardiser itself, which is a direct
reimplementation of the same step, fired in 3 of the 220 runs and in 3 of the 140 sequential
ones. In the other 137 it was handed a testbench that already observed every output and
already toggled its clock, and it returned that testbench unchanged.

The count is worth stating because the claim rests on it. Asserting that the standardiser
had nothing to do would otherwise be an inference from the absence of static findings, and
the standardiser runs before the analyser, so that inference would be circular.

The technique did not stop working. The defect it corrects stopped occurring.

That is a more useful conclusion than either a positive or a negative result on its own,
because it generalises past this project. Auxiliary tooling built to compensate for a
generator's weaknesses is calibrated against a particular generation of models, and its
value decays as those weaknesses are trained out. A pipeline that inherited AutoBench's
standardisation step unexamined would carry a component that costs maintenance and returns
nothing, and would have no way of knowing. The measurement, not the mechanism, is the
transferable part.

\section{Standing Against AutoBench}\label{sec:disc-autobench-compare}

The pipeline shares AutoBench's decomposition, its evaluation criteria and its benchmark, so
a direct comparison is the obvious question to ask. It is also the comparison this study is
least well designed to make, and the reasons are worth setting out before the numbers.

\paragraph{The benchmark subset is adversarially selected against us.} The twenty VerilogEval
problems used here were chosen for structural complexity before any run
(Section~\ref{sec:exp-circuits}), which was correct for RQ2 and is wrong for this comparison.
Their complexity scores run from 26.4 to 41.9 against a median of 18.7 across all 156, and
fifteen of the twenty are sequential where the full benchmark is 48\% sequential. AutoBench's
own figures show how much that matters: their Eval1 falls from 64.81\% on combinational
problems to 37.07\% on sequential ones. Weighting their published numbers to a 75\%
sequential mix gives roughly 44\% rather than 51.47\%, and that adjustment still does not
account for these being the hardest problems within the sequential class.

\paragraph{Eval2 is not measured the same way.} Ours is a raw detection rate over valid
mutants; theirs is agreement with a golden testbench at an 80\% threshold, counted over all
problems. Both are reported below, computed against AutoBench's own published mutants so that
at least the fault set is shared.

With the fourth sweep (Section~\ref{sec:res-strong}) the model difference is removed, and the
comparison becomes reportable for the first time, and Table~\ref{tab:vs-autobench} states it.

Against the sequential column, which is the fairer of the two, this pipeline reaches 50\%

at ceiling for both.

Three qualifications keep that from being a claim of superiority, and they are not
incidental.

The generators are two years apart. AutoBench used GPT-4-turbo; this sweep used
\texttt{claude-\allowbreak sonnet-4.5}. Section~\ref{sec:res-strong} shows what that
difference alone is worth on these circuits: Eval1 moved from 10\% to 25\% at
\texttt{baseline}, with no change to the pipeline whatsoever. A large part of any Eval1 advantage here is model progress, not
pipeline design, and this study cannot apportion it.

The subsets differ, and in both directions. Ours is harder within the benchmark, which
disadvantages us; it is also only twenty problems, so a Wilson interval on 10 of 20 runs from
30\% to 70\% and comfortably contains both of their figures. Twenty circuits cannot separate
these systems.

The Eval2 gap is the one that most likely reflects real capability. AutoBench runs scenario
checking with up to three retries and a reboot path with up to five, mechanisms this pipeline
does not have. That they detect more mutants while this pipeline passes Eval1 more often is
consistent with their system being tuned for coverage and this one for correctness.

The defensible statement is therefore that this pipeline is \emph{in the same range} as
AutoBench on their own benchmark's harder problems, with a newer model and fewer
self-enhancement mechanisms, and that no ranking between them is established by twenty
circuits. Section~\ref{sec:concl-future} sets out the run that would settle it: a randomly
drawn sample rather than the hardest quintile, with the \texttt{retry\_only} control present,
costing approximately \$23 on the telemetry in Section~\ref{sec:res-rq4}.

One narrower result stands without qualification. The 53.8\% detection rate the weaker
model's testbenches achieve on AutoBench's mutants, and 66.7\% for the stronger model's, set
against 97.4\% on our own fixtures, are measured on identical testbenches and identify the
fixture set --- not the mutant generator and not the testbenches --- as the reason Eval2
saturates in Section~\ref{sec:res-eval2}.

\section{Where Static Analysis Retains Value}\label{sec:disc-value}

The result should not be read as showing that pre-simulation analysis is useless in general.
Three qualifications limit its reach.

The three findings that did occur are informative about where the remaining risk sits. All
three were port-binding errors, and the two in the benchmark sweeps fell on the same circuit
--- the most port-dense in the selection, a twelve-port state machine. Structural errors did not vanish;
they became rare and concentrated in the circuits with the most structure to get wrong.
Designs far larger than anything tested here may reach a port count at which they return.

Smaller and locally-hosted models are the more likely case. This work tested two models from
two commercial providers, both well beyond the capability of a model a laboratory might run
on its own hardware. The claim that structural faults are rare is established for those two;
extending it to a quantised seven-billion-parameter model would require running the
experiment, and Section~\ref{sec:concl-future} proposes it.

The cost argument also survives. The layer is deterministic, requires no model call, and
completes in milliseconds; \texttt{pyverilog\_only} consumed 2\% fewer tokens than baseline.
A check that costs nothing and fires three times in 262 analyses is not obviously worth
removing, in the way that a smoke detector which has never sounded is not obviously worth
removing. What the result rules out is treating it as a primary defence.

\section{On Measurement Practice}\label{sec:disc-measurement}

The variance measurement in Section~\ref{sec:res-variance} was not an intended contribution.
It emerged because three configurations happened to execute identical logic, which made
their disagreement a direct reading of sampling noise: 33 percentage points, twice,
independently.

Two consequences follow for how this kind of work is evaluated. A comparison of
configurations at temperature 0.7 with a dozen circuits per arm cannot support a conclusion
about a difference smaller than roughly a third of the scale, and published results in this
area are routinely reported at that granularity without intervals. That figure is a property
of the sample size and not of the field: Section~\ref{sec:res-variance} shows it falling to
roughly 12 points across three arms at 44 circuits, and it must be rescaled before it is
carried anywhere else. AutoBench reports single-run pass@1 without intervals or repeats, and
at their 156 problems the one-arm standard deviation is roughly four points, so their 8\% and
10\% ablation gains sit at about two standard deviations of a single draw: suggestive, but
not separable from sampling variation without repeats.

The second consequence concerns controls. Any mechanism that triggers a regeneration must be
compared against uninformed regeneration, or the measured gain confounds the value of the
diagnosis with the value of the retry. Our own \texttt{hybrid} arm illustrates the point in
both directions: it leads the field at 40.9\%, and against the control that gap yields
$p=0.372$. Without \texttt{retry\_only} in the design we would have reported a 14-point
improvement over baseline and been wrong to.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:disc-threats}

\paragraph{Construct.} Eval2 is at ceiling at 324 of 335 mutants, 97\%, and discriminates
between no two configurations. Section~\ref{sec:res-eval2} tests the obvious explanation and
rejects it: regenerating the mutants with a stronger model under AutoBench's prompt, with
equivalent mutants filtered out, gives 97.4\%. The saturation is caused by the fixture
circuits being too small for a mutant to escape a finite stimulus, not by the generator. The
consequence is that Eval2 carries no weight in this report's conclusions, and that the
fixture set was the wrong instrument for it --- a design error, since the fixtures were
chosen in Section~\ref{sec:exp-circuits} to exercise the \emph{static checks}, and their
suitability for mutation testing was never considered. The failure categories in
Section~\ref{sec:res-rq1} come from keyword matching on model-authored scenario names, and
209 of 569 matched nothing, so they indicate shape rather than proportion.

\paragraph{Comparison.} The 20 benchmark problems were selected for structural complexity,
which biases them against this pipeline in any comparison with results reported over the full
156. The fourth sweep removes the model difference but not the selection bias, and twenty
circuits give a Wilson interval on \texttt{hybrid}'s Eval1 running from 30\% to 70\%, which
contains both of AutoBench's reported figures. Section~\ref{sec:disc-autobench-compare}
reports the comparison and states that no ranking is established by it.

\paragraph{Internal.} The driver prompt was tuned during development against six of the
twelve project fixtures, which are therefore not held out; the six purpose-built circuits
and all twenty benchmark problems were not used for tuning. The cross-model comparison
substitutes one commercial model for another, and the two differ in more than capability,
so attributing the observed behaviour to capability alone overstates what the design can
isolate.

The injection study has no held-out split either, and this is the more serious of the two.
The 93\% detection figure, the removal of \texttt{sensitivity\_list\_error} and the repair
that moved one class from 47\% to 100\% all come from the same 14 testbenches
(Section~\ref{sec:val-method}). The instrument was tuned and measured on one corpus, so the
figure is in-sample and should not be read as a prediction for unseen circuits.

\paragraph{Classification.} The circuit-type classifier was never measured. One error is
known: \texttt{Prob150\_\allowbreak review2015\_\allowbreak fsmonehot}, a combinational
next-state decoder, is labelled sequential, which is 1 of the 16 sequential labels assigned across the benchmark
sweeps. That label controls whether the standardiser runs and which checks apply, so its
accuracy bounds the analysis layer's, and Section~\ref{sec:val-limits} shows what a single
such error can do to a check that depends on it.

\paragraph{Coverage.} The strongest limit on the central null result is that the localiser
could not read 172 of the 434 testbenches it was given. Pyverilog rejects SystemVerilog
constructs the models emit, and the fallback provides a syntax verdict rather than an
analysis, so in those cases no check ran. The unanalysed share is worst on the benchmark
circuits under the weaker generator, at 89 of 153 --- the sweep whose circuits were selected
to give the localiser its best chance --- and improves to 48 of 120 under the stronger one,
which emits less SystemVerilog. The null therefore holds over the 262 analyses that ran, and
is silent about the rest. A localiser built on a parser with full SystemVerilog coverage would answer RQ2 on
a larger sample and might not answer it the same way; nothing in this study excludes that.

\paragraph{Analysis target.} The localiser was validated against reference designs and
deployed against generated ones. In all 280 runs the design it read was written by the same
model, from the same specification, in the same run as the testbench it was checked against,
while the score reported for that testbench came from compiling it against the golden design.
The six checks all test agreement between two artefacts, so co-generation is a plausible
cause of the null independent of any claim about what models get wrong.
Section~\ref{sec:disc-alternatives} states this as the one alternative explanation the study
does not exclude, and Section~\ref{sec:concl-future} gives the experiment that would.

\paragraph{External.} Twelve project circuits and twenty benchmark problems, two models,
one provider. The benchmark problems are drawn from HDLBits exercises and are small by
industrial standards. Nothing here speaks to testbenches for designs of realistic size.

\paragraph{Statistical.} Forty-four runs per configuration in the ablation, and twenty in the
benchmark sweep, leave the study underpowered for the comparison it was built to make; the
stratified result in Section~\ref{sec:res-strata} is post-hoc and uncorrected and is reported
as such. One
repeat per sweep was run; the budget did not allow more. Three sweeps converging on the same
qualitative picture is weaker evidence than three repeats of one sweep would have been for
the quantitative claim, and stronger evidence for the qualitative one.

\section{Corrections Made During the Work}\label{sec:disc-corrections}

Several defects in this work were found by the work itself, and the sequence is relevant to
how much confidence the results deserve.

Two defects in the localiser suppressed detection rather than causing false alarms. A parse
error caused by a missing trailing newline meant every testbench failed to parse and the
analysis returned an empty report, which is indistinguishable from a clean one; the
static-analysis arm was silently identical to the baseline for a full round of experiments.
Separately, checks that searched the raw source treated comments and string literals as
code, so a scenario named \texttt{addition\_boundary\_overflow} made an unchecked
\texttt{overflow} output appear observed. Detection for that class moved from 47\% to 100\%
when it was fixed.

Three defects were in the measurement harness rather than the pipeline, and all three would
have inflated the reported numbers. The injection corpus initially compiled mutated
testbenches against the pipeline's own generated design rather than the reference, and one
such design was malformed, so its compile error was recorded as a detection of the injected
fault. There was no gate requiring a corpus entry to pass before injection. A port-direction
parser silently dropped ports from shared declarations, causing injectors to skip them.

Two conclusions drawn during the work were retracted before they reached this report. The
completion of a sweep was announced on the basis of a file that the reporting script itself
creates. The cross-model hypothesis was described as confirmed on the strength of one
finding in nine analyses, where the final count was one in seventy-nine.

None of this is unusual for an empirical study; what would be unusual is a study of this
size in which nothing of the kind occurred. It is recorded because the figures in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} were produced by an instrument whose measured behaviour changed
during development, and a reader is entitled to know how much and in which direction.

\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{ch:conclusion}
% PHASE 6 · target 2.0 pp

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}\label{sec:concl-rq}

\textbf{RQ1.} Failures in LLM-generated testbenches are overwhelmingly semantic. Of 153
failing runs, 20 failed to compile and 133 compiled and then produced wrong results.
Re-simulating those 133 against the designs they were actually written for resolves 108 as
testbench faults, attributes 3 to a divergent design, and leaves 22 indeterminate because
the generated design does not elaborate (Section~\ref{sec:res-rq1}). The 569
individual failing scenarios concentrate on sequence and state behaviour, boundary
conditions, and reset and timing, all of which require knowing how the design behaves.
Establishing that any of them is wrong is not possible from the testbench text.

\textbf{RQ2.} Static analysis localises structural faults reliably and encounters them
rarely. It detects 93\% of injected faults, localises 93\% to the correct class and signal,
raises no false alarms on correct input, and catches 30 of the 33 faults that neither the
compiler nor the simulator can find. In 262 analyses of real generated testbenches it
reported findings on three runs; in a further 172 Pyverilog could not parse the testbench and
the checks did not run at all. A fourth sweep substituting the stronger generator on the
harder circuit set raised mean Eval1 across the three shared arms from 10\% to 35\% and left
the structural yield at one run in 120 analyses,
so the result is not an artefact of generator capability.

\textbf{RQ3.} The half of the question concerning repair guided by static analysis is
answered only in the weakest possible sense. In the 108 runs whose configuration permitted a
static finding to trigger a repair, the static path fired once, on the most port-dense
circuit in the study. The model acted on the diagnosis and the finding cleared; a subsequent
compiler-triggered regeneration reintroduced it, because the localiser reads the generated
design and evaluation compiles against the golden one, and for that circuit the two declare
different ports (Section~\ref{sec:res-strong}). One observation supports no rate, and the
honest position is that the mechanism was exercised once, under a configuration in which it
could not settle.

On the comparison between feedback strategies the answer is qualified but no longer empty.
Within either sample alone, no configuration is distinguishable from the no-diagnostics
control: the ablation gives $p=0.372$ unpaired and $p=0.125$ paired, the benchmark sweep
$p=0.219$. Across both, on 64 circuit-matched pairs, informed repair beats uninformed
regeneration on 11 circuits and loses on 2, and a stratified test returns $p=0.022$ --- a
post-hoc analysis that does not survive correction for the family of tests reported, and which
this work therefore reports as suggestive rather than established
(Section~\ref{sec:res-strata}).

Two subsidiary findings are firmer than that headline. First, uninformed regeneration is worth
approximately nothing: \texttt{retry\_only} fails to beat \texttt{baseline} in both samples
at $p=1.000$. Since it and \texttt{hybrid} both receive the extra generation attempt, whatever
separates them is the diagnosis. Second, the magnitude of what a diagnosis buys is small in
absolute terms: of \texttt{hybrid}'s 18 passes in the ablation, 3 were rescued by a repair and
15 passed first time against \texttt{baseline}'s 12 from an identical process, so the
mechanism is worth about 7 points and the remainder is variance. An ablation powered for a
7-point effect is what this question needed, and 44 runs per arm is not it.

What can be said about repair without an arm-level comparison is that it succeeds rarely and
that its success may depend on the generator: 3 of 23 diagnosed repairs with the mixed models
of the ablation, 5 of 15 with the stronger one, a difference not established at this sample
size. When it fails it does so in two ways --- the
diagnosis is not acted on, or each repair exchanges one failing corner case for another,
never repeating an error signature and never converging.

\textbf{RQ4.} Compiler feedback is the efficient choice: seven points of Eval1 above
baseline for 6\% more tokens. The full hybrid configuration buys the highest pass rate at
50\% more tokens and 45\% more wall time, without statistical support for the gap. Static
analysis costs 2\% fewer tokens than baseline, because its report was always clean and the
reasoning step was skipped.

\section{Contributions in Retrospect}\label{sec:concl-contrib}

Of the five contributions listed in Section~\ref{sec:intro-contrib}, the two that were not
planned have worn best. The control arm and the variance measurement were added to make the
ablation defensible, and they ended up producing the results most likely to be useful
elsewhere: a demonstration that uninformed regeneration can degrade a testbench that already
compiled when the generator is weak enough, and a figure for how large a difference has to be before it means anything at this
sample size.

The sixth contribution claimed in the project plan, per-node attribution of failure origin,
was not delivered. The pipeline records the stage at which failure is detected, and 70\% of
runs attribute to the evaluation node for the straightforward reason that evaluation is
where failure becomes visible. Recovering where a defect was introduced would require
ground truth per node that the pipeline does not collect. What exists instead is the
repair-feedback breakdown, which shows 42 repairs triggered by simulation, 8 by the
compiler, and none by static analysis.

The result that generalises furthest is not about static analysis at all. AutoBench's
largest reported gain came from a script inserting output-display statements that
GPT-4-turbo omitted. Two years later the equivalent check in this work fired zero times in
262 analyses, and the deterministic standardiser built for the same purpose had something to
insert in 3 of 220 runs. The technique did not stop working; the defect it corrects stopped
occurring.
Auxiliary tooling built around a generator's weaknesses is calibrated against a particular
generation of models, and a component inherited from prior work without re-measurement may
cost maintenance and return nothing.

\section{Future Work}\label{sec:concl-future}

\textbf{Re-analyse against the golden design.} The localiser read the generated design in
every run while evaluation compiled against the golden one
(Section~\ref{sec:disc-alternatives}), so the null it reports is a null about co-generated
artefacts. Re-running the frozen localiser over every stored testbench paired with its
reference design answers this from data already on disk, deterministically and with no model
calls. If the yield stays near zero the central claim is materially stronger; if it rises,
the yield was suppressed by what the localiser was shown rather than by what the models
write. This is the first thing that should be done and the cheapest.

\textbf{Smaller and locally-hosted models.} The claim that structural faults are rare rests
on two commercial models. A quantised open-weight model running on local hardware is the
case where the checks might still earn their place, and the experiment is inexpensive: the
pipeline is provider-agnostic and the sweep costs approximately \$0.50 at the cheaper tier.

\textbf{The two classes the boundary excluded.} Section~\ref{sec:val-limits} measures two
candidate checks that reach the negative-control classes on the injection corpus and finds
that neither would have produced a correct finding on any of the 280 real runs. Pursuing them
is therefore not proposed. What the exercise does suggest is a different target: the
classification stage, whose single sequential-versus-combinational error was enough to
generate three false positives from an otherwise sound check. A check is only as reliable as
the context it is given, and no part of this study measures the accuracy of that context.

\textbf{Semantic checking.} The defects that dominate are semantic, and static analysis
cannot reach them from the testbench alone. It could reach some of them given the
specification: generating assertions from the structured specification the pipeline already
extracts, and checking the testbench's expectations against those assertions, would attack
the class that matters rather than the class that is convenient.

\textbf{A representative benchmark comparison.} The single most valuable missing experiment,
and the cheapest. The benchmark subset here is the hardest quintile of VerilogEval and was run
with the cheaper model, so Section~\ref{sec:disc-autobench-compare} can state no relative
standing against AutoBench. A baseline sweep over a randomly drawn sample of the 156 at the
stronger tier costs approximately \$2.20 by the telemetry in Section~\ref{sec:res-rq4}, and
running both that sample and the existing hard subset would separate pipeline capability from
circuit difficulty rather than confounding them.

\textbf{Larger circuits for Eval2.} Mutant quality has been eliminated as the cause of the
Eval2 ceiling (Section~\ref{sec:res-eval2}); circuit size has not. The measure discriminates
on the benchmark circuits, where the same testbenches detect 53.8\%, and saturates on
fixtures with two ports and one input bit. Re-running Eval2 over designs with enough state
for a defect to escape a finite stimulus would make it informative, and requires no new
tooling.

\textbf{Repair that converges.} Section~\ref{sec:res-repair-fail} shows that 13 of 20 failed
repairs exhausted their budget while producing a different error signature at every
iteration, exchanging one corner case for another rather than converging. Two directions
follow: retaining the failing scenarios already fixed as explicit constraints on the next
regeneration, so that repair accumulates rather than resamples; and patching the testbench
rather than regenerating it whole, which is what exposes the pipeline to the degradation
measured in Section~\ref{sec:res-retry}.

\textbf{Statistical power.} Section~\ref{sec:res-strata} leaves the central comparison
suggestive and uncorrected at $p=0.022$ over 64 matched pairs. Settling it requires a
pre-registered replication rather than more post-hoc pooling: \texttt{baseline},
\texttt{retry\_only} and \texttt{hybrid} over a randomly drawn benchmark sample, with the
hypothesis and the test fixed in advance. Section~\ref{sec:res-decompose} sharpens the target
--- the mechanism rescued 3 runs in 44, so the experiment must be powered for a 7-point
effect, not the 13.6-point raw gap. At the stronger tier a 60-problem three-arm sweep costs
approximately \$23 by the telemetry in Section~\ref{sec:res-rq4}.
